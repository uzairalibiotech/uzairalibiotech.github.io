About

A bit about my work

Research interest Neurometabolic regulation: the biology connecting metabolic reprogramming and neuronal signalling. In plainer terms: I like figuring out how to turn white fat brown, and how neurons decide when to fire.

I'm a research scientist working at the junction of metabolic and neuro biology. Most of my published work focuses on adipocytes, particularly how they switch between energy storing and energy burning states, and how that connects to metabolic disease. Alongside this, my postdoctoral work on neuromodulation gave me hands on experience with primary neuronal assays, viral gene delivery, and calcium imaging in neurons. My toolkit spans cell culture, plasmid and viral vector gene delivery, gene expression analysis, confocal and calcium imaging, and the statistical and bioinformatic analysis that turns those experiments into a story.

I've worked in international, collaborative research environments across Italy, Sweden, Finland, and Germany, coordinating projects and supervising junior researchers along the way. Since mid 2025 I've been building an independent research and consulting practice: peer reviewing for international journals, giving invited lectures on research careers and international academic pathways, and staying engaged with the wider scientific community through conferences and continued training.

A red protein in brown fat: a critical look at a new paper on adipocyte myoglobin and energy expenditure

Another paper landed in my feed this week that sits even closer to my own PhD work than the last one I wrote about: Strehlau et al., in Advanced Science, on myoglobin as a determinant of energy expenditure in fat cells. My thesis was built around exactly this question, what transcriptional and functional programs let an adipocyte behave more like a furnace than a storage tank. This paper approaches it through an unexpected door: a protein everyone associates with muscle, showing up inside brown and beige fat cells and apparently doing real metabolic work there.

The starting observation is that myoglobin (MB), classically known for storing and shuttling oxygen in muscle, is also expressed in brown and beige adipocytes, where cold exposure and thermogenic activation raise its levels. Since whole body myoglobin knockout mice cannot separate the contribution of fat-expressed MB from muscle and heart-expressed MB, the authors built a new mouse line, an adipose tissue-specific MB knockout (ATMBKO), using Cre recombinase under the adiponectin promoter. In these mice, losing MB only in fat lowered whole body energy expenditure, blunted body temperature during cold exposure, and increased weight gain and body fat on a high fat diet, all while muscle and heart MB stayed completely normal. Mechanistically, MB appears to work as a lipid-binding shuttle that helps fatty acids reach the mitochondria for oxidation, and losing that shuttle left more fat sitting in the tissue and more triglycerides circulating in the blood instead of being burned.

What convinced me. The cleanest thing about this paper is the genetic logic. Earlier myoglobin knockout work always carried a confound, since deleting MB everywhere in the body cannot tell you whether a metabolic effect comes from fat, muscle, or heart. Building the adipose-specific knockout, and confirming by antibody staining that muscle and heart MB were untouched, closes that gap directly. On top of that, the mechanistic dissection is genuinely careful. The Seahorse fatty acid oxidation assays separate long chain fatty acids, which need MB and CPT1A-mediated shuttling into the mitochondria, from medium chain fatty acids, which can diffuse in on their own, and MB loss only hurts the long chain substrates, exactly where the proposed shuttle should matter. Then, to check whether the effect is specifically about MB's ability to bind lipids rather than just its presence, they repeat the human adipocyte experiments with a lipid-binding-dead mutant that expresses at similar levels but fails to rescue lipolysis or respiration. That is a nicely controlled way of asking whether the binding function itself is doing the work, not just the protein's presence.

Where I would push back. The strongest causal claim in the paper, that restoring MB is enough to reverse the metabolic phenotype, does not actually use the tissue-specific model the rest of the paper is built around. The knockout side of the story runs entirely on ATMBKO mice, adipose-specific deletion, muscle and heart untouched. But the rescue experiment switches to a different animal altogether: whole body MBKO mice, which lack MB everywhere, injected locally with an MB-expressing plasmid directly over the interscapular BAT. That is a reasonable practical choice, since re-injecting plasmid into a specific fat depot of an already adipose-specific knockout would not add much new information, but it does mean the loss-of-function and gain-of-function halves of the argument were never demonstrated in the same genetic background.

A second point worth sitting with is how the "contractile brown adipocyte" subcluster is identified. The claim that MB marks a muscle-like, high-UCP1 brown adipocyte population, and that this population shrinks when MB is lost, rests on combining marker genes from a public single nuclei RNA sequencing dataset with a score computed on the authors' own bulk RNA-seq data. That is a legitimate and increasingly common approach, but it is still a deconvolution-style inference layered on bulk tissue, not a direct single nuclei sequencing of the ATMBKO animals themselves, which would show more directly whether that subcluster actually shrinks or simply looks less muscle-like on average. I would treat the contractile-subcluster story as a strong supporting hypothesis rather than a directly demonstrated cellular shift. It is also worth noting that the deepest mechanistic work, the RNA-seq, proteomics, and most of the cold-exposure metabolomics, was done in male mice only, while the headline body weight and thermoregulation phenotypes were confirmed in both sexes. That is a common and understandable practical constraint, but it leaves open whether the underlying molecular mechanism looks identical in females. Finally, several figures rely on Fisher's LSD post hoc testing rather than a more conservative multiple comparison correction, a defensible choice for a hypothesis-driven figure with few comparisons, but worth knowing when reading the significance stars at face value.

Why it matters beyond the mouse cage. The translational case here is stronger than most adipose biology papers I read, mainly because the human data is not an afterthought. MB shows up in human iPSC-derived brown adipocytes, in primary supraclavicular brown fat, and overexpressing it in primary human white adipocytes increases both lipolysis and mitochondrial respiration, using the same lipid-binding-dead mutant as a control. The framing I found most useful is the positioning against current anti-obesity drugs. GLP-1 receptor agonists and other incretin-based therapies work mostly by reducing how much people eat, but say very little about how much energy a person's tissue burns. A pathway that increases adipose energy expenditure, rather than intake, is mechanistically distinct, and the paper explicitly frames MB as a potential complement rather than a competitor to those drugs.

To put a number on how much worse the thermoregulatory deficit gets under combined metabolic stress: on a chow diet, chronic cold exposure alone produced about a half degree body temperature deficit in ATMBKO mice compared to littermates. Add a high fat diet and acute cold together, and that deficit roughly doubles.

Where this connects to my own work. My PhD was built around exactly this kind of question, what transcriptional and functional programs let an adipocyte act more like a furnace than a storage tank, and this paper adds a specific molecular player I had not previously had reason to think about: a heme protein borrowed from muscle biology, doing lipid-shuttling work inside fat. It also connects unexpectedly to my first author work on capsaicin, camphor, and caffeic acid promoting lipolysis through TRPV1. This paper found that MB is not induced by direct adrenergic stimulation in vitro, canonical thermogenic signals like norepinephrine failed to raise MB levels on their own, and instead pinned thyroid hormone as the dominant upstream driver. That leaves an open question I had not considered before: whether TRPV1-active compounds influence adipocyte thermogenic capacity partly through this thyroid hormone-MB axis, rather than purely through the calcium and adrenergic signaling I focused on in that paper. Nothing in either paper tests that directly, but it is exactly the kind of connection that makes reading outside one's own narrow dataset worthwhile.

Overall, this is a carefully built genetic model answering a real gap in the field, adipose-specific deletion succeeding where whole body knockouts could not separate tissue contributions, backed by a reasonably thorough mechanistic and translational case. The main thing I will be watching for in follow-up work is exactly the loop I flagged above: a BAT-specific MB rescue performed in the ATMBKO line itself, rather than in a separate whole body knockout, which would make the causal argument airtight rather than merely convincing.

Strehlau, C., Broghammer, H., Gebhardt, C., et al. (2026). Adipocyte Myoglobin Is a Determinant of Energy Expenditure and a Potential Target to Limit Obesity. Advanced Science. doi.org/10.1002/advs.76191

Reading between the contact sites: a critical look at a new EMBO Journal paper on adipocyte organelle architecture

A paper landed in my feed this week that sits close to home: Palard et al., in The EMBO Journal, on how the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria and lipid droplets physically reorganize inside adipocytes depending on whether the cell is fed, fasted, or dysfunctional. My own PhD spent three years staring at adipocyte gene expression networks trying to explain how a cell decides to store fat or burn it. This paper approaches the same question from a different angle entirely, not what genes turn on, but where the organelles sit relative to each other. I wanted to sit with it a bit longer than a usual field note, both because the biology is genuinely elegant and because a few parts of the study deserve a more skeptical read.

The core idea is that adipocytes do not just store triglycerides in lipid droplets and leave it there. Three types of organelle contact sites, between the ER and mitochondria (known as MAMs), between mitochondria and lipid droplets, and between the ER and lipid droplets directly, rearrange constantly depending on nutrient state. The authors focus especially on a subset of MAMs that sit close to lipid droplets, which they call MAM-LD, as opposed to MAM-CM, MAMs involving mitochondria that are not near a droplet. Using mice lacking the ER protein Seipin in adipocytes, alongside 3T3-L1 cell models, they show that feeding normally shifts the balance toward MAM-LD and lipid-droplet-facing contacts, while Seipin loss collapses this entire remodeling capacity and blunts triglyceride delivery into the droplet itself.

To make that shift concrete: in control tissue, the paper reports MAM-CM and MAM-LD contacts split roughly evenly, while in Seipin-deficient tissue that balance swings hard toward MAM-CM, with MAM-LD reduced to a small minority of total contacts.

What convinced me. The strongest part of this paper, to me, is not any single figure, it is the layering. The authors do not rely on one imaging technique and call it a day. Transmission electron microscopy, proximity ligation assay combined with structured illumination microscopy, and full 3D FIB-SEM reconstruction all point the same direction independently, and then they back the structural claims with a functional readout, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, to show that fewer contacts actually mean less lipid getting into the droplet, not just a prettier picture. Even better, they do not stop at correlation. Reintroducing an artificial ER-mitochondria tether, Linker-ER-Mi, rescues the lipid transfer defect in Seipin-deficient cells, and knocking down the mitochondrial calcium uniporter on top of that rescue abolishes it again. That is a genuinely convincing causal chain: structure changes, function changes, restoring structure restores function, and blocking a specific downstream signal removes the rescue. Having spent time doing RT-qPCR and calcium imaging myself during my postdoc, I recognize how much optimization sits behind getting a clean IP3-triggered calcium trace. This is not trivial work.

Where I would push back. A few things gave me pause on a closer read. The in vivo electron microscopy comparisons are drawn from two mice per genotype, with five cells analyzed per mouse. That is a common constraint in TEM work, since the technique is slow and expensive, but it does mean the biological variability across a real animal population is not fully captured, and the reported statistics mostly describe variation between individual contact sites rather than between independent animals. The proximity ligation assay data, by contrast, is drawn from far more animals and cells, which is reassuring, but the two datasets are measuring related, not identical, things, and I would have liked a clearer statement of how much the electron microscopy conclusions specifically hinge on those two animals per group.

There is also a classification choice worth sitting with. Whether a mitochondrion counts as peri-droplet (and its ER contact therefore counts as MAM-LD) versus cytosolic depends on a distance cutoff, 50 nanometers in the electron microscopy data, 380 nanometers in the proximity ligation and structured illumination data, with the second number chosen because that is roughly the physical size of the antibody-based signal complex itself, not because of any independently established biological boundary. The authors are upfront about this, which I respect, but it means the striking swing shown above is, to some extent, a function of where that line is drawn. A modest shift in threshold could plausibly compress or exaggerate that swing. None of this undermines the direction of the finding, but it is worth remembering that MAM-LD versus MAM-CM is currently an operational definition, not yet a molecularly distinct category with its own marker set.

The proximity ligation assay itself is also, by design, a proxy. It reports how close two proteins sit to each other, inferred from an antibody-based signal, not a literal membrane-to-membrane contact visualized directly, and the authors are careful to use that "proxy" language themselves. Combined with the fact that the inducible Seipin knockout mice were analyzed only about two weeks after gene deletion, a stage the authors describe as capturing early, mild dysfunction before more severe fat loss sets in, I think the honest summary is that this paper nails down an early, upstream mechanism convincingly, but leaves the later, more clinically established stage of lipodystrophy comparatively unexplored. Similarly, FATE1, the protein used to artificially force MAMs apart, is normally expressed in testis, not fat, so overexpressing it in adipocytes is a clean tool for testing what happens when MAMs disappear, but it is an engineered perturbation rather than something that happens naturally in a dysfunctional adipocyte. I would treat those experiments as proof of concept for causality rather than a model of any real pathology. And as with most of this field, the cell line workhorse here is 3T3-L1, a well characterized but still imperfect stand-in for human or even primary mouse adipocytes.

Why it matters beyond a rare disease. Seipin deficiency causes congenital generalized lipodystrophy, a severe and rare condition, so on its own this could read as a niche mechanism. What elevates the paper is the parallel experiment in ordinary diet-induced obese mice. Lean mice show the expected nutrient-driven remodeling, MAMs drop and lipid-droplet-facing contacts rise after feeding, and this flexibility is essentially gone in obese mice. That lines up with a growing body of work on adipose tissue metabolic inflexibility, the idea that obese fat loses its capacity to adjust storage and mobilization to the fed or fasted state, and it gives that concept a concrete organelle-level home. The detail I found most useful, and the one I think is easy to miss on a first read, is the paper's own conclusion in the discussion: permanently locking MAMs together with a synthetic tether like Linker-ER-Mi is explicitly framed as an unpromising therapeutic direction, precisely because the biology here is about the capacity to remodel, not a fixed optimal contact number. The more interesting therapeutic target the authors point to is something that restores flexible remodeling, or that transiently boosts MAM-LD specifically around the postprandial window, rather than a permanent structural fix. That is a more nuanced and more defensible position than simply increasing MAM contacts to treat obesity, and I think it deserves to be the headline takeaway rather than a line buried at the end of the discussion.

Where this connects to my own work. My PhD approached adipocyte browning almost entirely from the transcriptional side, using RNA-seq and network pharmacology to find regulators of the switch between energy-storing and energy-burning states. This paper is a useful complement, since it shows that the same functional switch also has a physical, organellar signature, mitochondria and the ER visibly repositioning around lipid droplets as the cell shifts state. It also connects to my first author work on capsaicin, camphor and caffeic acid promoting lipolysis through TRPV1 involvement. Reading this paper left me with an open question I had not considered before: whether lipolytic agonists like these act, even partially, by shifting the balance of organelle contact sites rather than purely through canonical signaling cascades. Nothing in this paper tests that directly, but the lipolysis assays here, using a beta-adrenergic agonist and showing that disrupting MAMs blunts glycerol release, suggest that organelle architecture and lipolytic capacity are more tightly coupled than I had previously given credit for.

Overall, I think this is a well constructed piece of cell biology, with the causal rescue experiments doing a lot of the heavy lifting to justify the model, and a discussion section that is refreshingly honest about its own limits, including the admitted lack of live imaging of the tripartite contact itself and insufficient yield to characterize the MAM-LD proteome directly. The open question I will be watching for next is exactly that: what MAM-LD actually looks like at the protein level, and whether it turns out to be a genuinely distinct organelle contact category or simply the lipid-droplet-proximal end of a single MAM continuum.

Palard, M., Chadeuf, G., Carpentier, M., et al. (2026). Tripartite ER-mitochondria-lipid droplets contact sites control adipocyte metabolic flexibility. The EMBO Journal. doi.org/10.1038/s44318-026-00876-z

Why I'm starting this journal
